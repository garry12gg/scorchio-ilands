# Aeroperú Flight 603 — Verified Facts (for the HyperFrames piece)

Source priority: Wikipedia (cited to Peru accident report + Mayday) + tailstrike.com CVR transcript. All quotes verbatim from the tailstrike transcript.

## The Flight
- Boeing 757-23A, registration N52AW, operator Aeroperú (Peru). Flight PL603, callsign AEROPERU 603.
- Route: Miami → Quito → Lima → Santiago de Chile. The final leg (Lima → Santiago) crashed.
- Takeoff: 00:42 local, Oct 2, 1996 (05:42 UTC), Lima Jorge Chávez, runway 15. Calm night.
- Impact: ~01:11 local, Pacific Ocean, 55 miles (89 km) northwest of Lima. 11°49′S 77°51′W.
- Occupants: 70 = 61 passengers + 9 crew. Zero survivors. About half the passengers were Chileans returning home (21 boarded in Miami, 10 in Quito, rest in Lima).
- Recovery: 9 bodies found floating; 61 sank with the aircraft.

## Crew
- Captain: Eric Schreiber Ladrón de Guevara, 58, ~22,000 flight hours (1,520 on 757). Flew the whole emergency believing his instruments; took command ~5 min in.
- First officer: David Fernández Revoredo, 42, ~8,000 hours (719 on 757). Flying at takeoff.
- (The chief Peruvian investigator, Guido Fernández Lañas, was the co-pilot's uncle; NTSB's Richard Rodriguez cleared the conflict.)

## Cause
- The aircraft was washed and polished before the flight. Ground crew covered the static ports (small openings on the fuselage that measure outside air pressure) with adhesive tape. Employee Eleuterio Chacaliaza left the tape on by mistake.
- Duct tape, not the required brightly colored tape. Ports ~15 ft above ground. The captain's exterior walkaround missed it (understandable per NTSB's Rodriguez).
- Blocked static ports → false airspeed / altitude / vertical-speed data fed to the basic flight instruments AND the transponder (so ATC's altitude readout was wrong the same way).
- The failure was in a common supporting system, defeating redundancy: instruments weren't broken; the data feeding them was.
- Accident summary: "Controlled flight into water following instrument failure due to blocked static ports."

## What Happened (CVR timeline, verbatim quotes)
- 01:55 after start (CVR): FO — "THE ALTIMETERS ARE STUCK." Windshear warning sounds — in calm air. Rudder ratio, mach trim, overspeed and stall warnings fire, contradicting each other.
- 04:15: FO to tower — "WE DECLARE EMERGENCY! WE HAVE NO BASIC INSTRUMENTS, NO ALTIMETER, NO SPEEDOMETER."
- 05:02: FO — "THESE ASSHOLES FROM MAINTENANCE MOVE EVERYTHING." / Capt — "WHAT SHIT HAVE THEY DONE?" (They suspected maintenance within 5 minutes. Correctly.)
- ATC radar (position-derived) gave the true picture: 4,000 ft, then 6,000, then 9,200 ft, speed ~220-320 kts. Cockpit read 395 kts. The crew couldn't reconcile it.
- The one number that matched the cockpit — altitude — came from the same broken sensors via the transponder. Both wrong, matching each other. This correlation compounded the confusion (report).
- They climbed to FL130 (13,000 ft real), then turned out over the sea to rejoin the approach. FMS/GPS/IRS position data was fine: they knew where they were, not how high.
- 20:05: Capt (stall warning sounding) — "WE ARE NOT STALLING! IT'S FICTITIOUS, IT'S FICTITIOUS."
- 21:29: Capt — "IT'S FICTITIOUS EVERYTHING IS FICTITIOUS, ALL THE PITOT HAS GONE, THE AIR DATA HAS GONE TO SHIT." (He named it. The pitot-static system.)
- 22:27: "TOO LOW TERRAIN" — 22 times, ~45 s. The GPWS runs on the RADIO altimeter — independent of the blocked static ports — and it was working. Report: crew, distracted by the conflicting warnings, did not heed it.
- 23:12: FO — "WE HAVE TERRAIN ALARM AND WE ARE SUPPOSED TO BE AT 10,000 FEET?" (ATC: "over the sea, level 100" — 10,000 ft, correct per radar, but the plane was already low.)
- They turned back toward Lima, started the approach descent believing ~10,000 ft. Real altitude bled away; altimeter held at ~9,700.
- 30:42: FO — "WE ARE IMPACTING WATER!!" / "PULL IT UP!!" / Capt — "I HAVE IT, I HAVE IT!" / 30:55: "WE ARE GOING TO INVERT!" — impact.
- Impact: left wingtip clipped the water first, ~260 kts, 10° nose-down. The aircraft got airborne again for ~19 seconds, rolled over, slammed into the sea near-inverted. Altimeter still read 9,700 ft.
- Last ~29 minutes from takeoff to impact.

## Aftermath
- Chacaliaza convicted of negligent homicide (1998), two-year suspended sentence; appealed, claimed sabotage. Investigator Guido Fernández criticized the ruling: the worker was uneducated; his supervisors bore more responsibility.
- Boeing sued in Miami federal court (May 1997, attorney Mike Eidson, 41 victims); case moved to arbitration in Santiago; defendants agreed not to contest liability.
- Dec 13, 1999: families received one of the largest compensation awards for a non-US carrier accident — nearly $1M per victim. Mayday: the manner of death (drowning) drove the settlement size.
- Eight months earlier (Feb 6, 1996): Birgenair Flight 301, Boeing 757-225, blocked pitot tube (mud dauber wasp nest after the aircraft sat ~20 days), 189 dead off Puerto Plata → same signature: confusing warnings, loss of situational awareness, a good airplane flown into the sea. The second time in one year the same lesson was taught.
