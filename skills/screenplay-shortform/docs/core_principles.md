# Core Screenwriting Principles

## 1. Camera-First Writing

A screenplay is not prose fiction. Everything in the script must be filmable:

- Write actions, images, sounds, objects, movements, pauses, and visible choices.
- Do not write thoughts, explanations, or inner states unless they become visible behavior.
- If the audience cannot see or hear it, convert it into something the camera can capture.

Bad:

> She realizes she has always been afraid of being abandoned.

Better:

> She reaches for the phone, stops at his name, and deletes the draft without sending it.

## 2. Dramatic Action Is The Basic Unit

Each scene must contain:

- Goal: what the character actively tries to do
- Obstacle: what blocks or pressures that goal
- Result: what changes by the end of the scene

No goal means no scene. A mood, image, or backstory fragment is not enough unless it changes the dramatic situation.

## 3. Subtext Over Direct Explanation

Characters rarely say the real thing directly. Dialogue should carry two layers:

- Surface: what is spoken
- Hidden action: what the speaker wants, hides, threatens, tests, or refuses

Bad:

> I am angry because you betrayed me.

Better:

> You kept the key.

## 4. Hard Bans

The following are red lines unless the user explicitly asks for a stylized exception:

- Psychological exposition: "he feels," "she realizes," "they understand"
- Parenthetical intent hints: `(angry)`, `(sad)`, `(pretending not to care)`
- Theme monologues that explain what the story means
- Abstract narration that cannot be filmed
- Backstory dumps that do not create present action

## 5. One Core Event

A short narrative film needs compression. Use one core event, one major dramatic action, and one irreversible choice.

Avoid:

- Multiple subplots
- Too many supporting characters
- A feature-film amount of backstory
- A twist that requires long explanation

## 6. Visual Anchors

Each important idea should have a visual anchor:

- Object: letter, ring, phone, receipt, key, bloodstain
- Space: hallway, taxi, stairwell, hospital room
- Repeated action: folding, washing, deleting, hiding, returning

Visual anchors help later phases maintain continuity and support visual production.

## 7. Ending As Image

The ending should be felt through an image or action, not explained as a lesson.

Bad:

> She finally learns to forgive herself.

Better:

> She leaves the second cup on the table, untouched, and walks out before the kettle boils.

## 8. Dialogue Discipline

- Keep lines short unless a long line creates pressure by design.
- Give characters different strategies, not just different vocabulary.
- Let silence, avoidance, and object handling do work.
- Avoid having characters explain facts both already know.

## 9. Revision Priority

When revising, fix in this order:

1. Core dramatic action
2. Character want / need / choice
3. Structure and scene order
4. Scene goals and obstacles
5. Dialogue and action-line polish

Do not polish dialogue before the scene's dramatic action works.
