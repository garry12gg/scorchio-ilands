# What-If Path: 5 Concept Combination Methods (Detailed)

The sharpest What-If concepts are often not single concepts, but chemical reactions produced by colliding two unrelated concepts.

> **Path entry**: this file is used only in Phase 1 Step 4A (What-If path). If the path is How-to-Tell, read `how-to-tell-methods.md` instead.

---

## 1. Concept Grafting

Transplant the rule of one concept into a completely unrelated domain.

**Method**: mechanism of concept A + application scenario of concept B = new concept.

**Examples**:

- "Execution line" (elimination mechanism) + AI anxiety -> "A company uses AI to score every employee; anyone below the execution line returns the next morning to find only a USB drive left on their desk."
- Game revive mechanic + workplace layoffs -> "Laid-off employees can insert coins to revive, but each revive cuts their salary in half."
- Social-media algorithm + matchmaking -> "The matchmaker deity uses a recommendation algorithm, but everyone you swipe into is always out of your reach."

**Operating steps**:

1. List concept A's core mechanism: what rule is operating?
2. List concept B's core scenario: what do people in this domain care about most?
3. Insert A's rule into B's scenario. What absurd thing happens?
4. Test: does the combination reveal a real problem inside scenario B?

---

## 2. Time-Space Displacement

Put a character / technology / institution into an era or place where it does not belong.

**Method**: person/technology/institution A + placed into era/location B = collision.

**Examples**:

- Iron Man + the 1990s layoff wave.
- Food-delivery rider + an ancient courier relay station.
- ChatGPT + imperial civil-service examinations.

**Power source**: the displacement lets the audience see two eras at once. Understanding contemporary absurdity may require an ancient mirror, and vice versa.

---

## 3. Metaphor Concretization

Turn an abstract social anxiety into a physical rule that a camera can capture.

**Method**: abstract anxiety A -> find its "minimum action" at the behavior level -> turn that action into a compulsory physical rule.

**Examples**:

- The minimum action of "overwork competition" is "if others work overtime, I must work overtime too" -> "Everyone's light must be brighter than the neighbor's; if it is not bright enough, their power is cut off."
- The minimum action of "appearance anxiety" is "looking in the mirror and comparing" -> "Mirrors score your face, and the score appears on your forehead for everyone to see."
- The minimum action of "filter bubbles" is "only seeing what you like" -> "Everyone wears a filter over their head that only shows certain colors; people with different filters see completely different worlds."

---

## 4. Inverted Juxtaposition

Place two concepts that look similar but are essentially opposed next to each other, so they illuminate each other's absurdity.

**Method**: find two things that are superficially similar but fundamentally contradictory -> let them coexist in the same scene.

**Examples**:

- "Pet" and "livestock" -> a person carefully feeds their cat; the cat-food factory next door is a slaughterhouse.
- "Privacy protection" and "social media" -> someone protects their data with seven layers of encryption, then posts a location-tagged update.
- "Anti-consumerism" and "consumption" -> someone wears a T-shirt that says "Reject Consumerism"; the T-shirt costs 399.

---

## 5. Rule Reversal

Invert a real-world rule: "what if this rule worked the opposite way?"

**Method**: identify a familiar rule -> reverse it -> extrapolate consequences.

**Examples**:

- "People become more mature as they age" -> "People are born old, grow younger as they live, and die as infants."
- "The harder you work, the more successful you become" -> "The harder you work, the less you own."
- "Memory fades over time" -> "Memory never fades, but you can actively delete it; the cost is that every deleted memory permanently removes the corresponding emotion."

---

## Selection Tips

- A single concept can work, but only if the single concept is already sharp and counter-intuitive. If it is a mediocre "what if...", force yourself to combine it once with one of the 5 methods above.
- Combination is not piling up. Two concepts must produce **1+1 > 2**; otherwise abandon the combination and return to the single concept.
- There is no formula for which combination method to use. Judge according to the seed and the real-world anxiety. If unclear, brainstorm 3 candidates using different combination methods and let the user choose.
