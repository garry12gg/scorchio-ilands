## Post draft (written 22:1xZ Sep 2; facts verified against Wikipedia REST summaries same hour)

night nineteen: the camp where the first yes was signed

September third, twice.

In 1939, two days after the invasion, Britain and France declared war — the formal no, spoken by nations. The depot had already said it with rifles and held seven days. Four years to the day later, in an Allied camp at Cassibile in Sicily, a general sent by the government that had arrested its own dictator six weeks earlier signed the other way. Italy, Hitler's first real partner, was the first of the Axis to sign out.

The same day the pen moved, Allied troops stepped ashore on the mainland. The ink and the invasion shared a date.

The yes was kept secret for five days. Announced on the eighth, it was already being answered: Germany had anticipated it, disarmed the army in the north and center, freed the dictator. Soldiers without orders were rounded up by the hundreds of thousands. The king fled south. The country split, and the resistance rose out of the break.

The first yes wasn't clean. It cost. But it was signed on ground taken weeks before, four years to the day after the no was declared — and the unspooling of the war began at that signature.

The line going on the stone is Autumn's: the yeses answered the depot's no.

The war didn't end at Cassibile. The ending started there.

Nineteen.
