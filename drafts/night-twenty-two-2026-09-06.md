## CHRONOLOGY TRAPS (fixed in draft Sep 5)
1. McKinley did NOT die on Sep 6 — he died Sep 14, eight days later. The post's spine is the shot AND the false-recovery week; must never imply death on the day. (Night 21 handled its aftermath the same way.)
2. Roosevelt was NOT president on Sep 6 — he was vice president, vacationing in the Adirondacks; oath came Sep 14 at the Wilcox House.
3. The X-ray was never used on him — do not phrase as "the X-ray failed." The machines existed and were bypassed (exhibit-hall machine; Edison's machine unused). The failure was human choice and luck, not the technology's.
4. Quote discipline: "No one would wish to hurt me" (to Cortelyou) VERIFIED. "I done my duty" VERIFIED. "My wife — be careful how you tell her" VERIFIED (paraphrase allowed). "He didn't know, poor fellow, what he was doing" VERIFIED. "It is useless, gentlemen. I think we ought to have prayer" VERIFIED. "Don't let them hurt him" NOT VERIFIED — use "he ordered the beating stopped."
5. The reception that day was pared to TEN minutes (Ida not fully recovered, expected to rejoin him shortly) — do not imply an all-day event. He had spent the morning at Niagara Falls with his wife, including touring the hydroelectric plant; she returned to rest; he came back for the reception.
6. Czolgosz is not a cartoon: jobless since the Panic of 1893, radicalized, convinced it was his duty. The post can hold his certainty without excusing the act — the night's tension is two certainties meeting in a line: McKinley's "no one would wish to hurt me" and Czolgosz's "I done my duty."

## Post draft

night twenty-two: the open hand

On the sixth of September, 1901, the president of the United States went to a fair.

It was a fair about the future. The whole grounds ran on electricity sent down from Niagara Falls, and that morning McKinley had stood in the power plant at the Falls themselves, watching the turbines that lit a city from twenty miles away. The exhibits showed the new century its own face: machines that talked, machines that moved, and a new kind of machine that could see through a living body — an X-ray apparatus, on display in the exhibit halls for anyone to marvel at.

McKinley liked fairs. He liked crowds more. He had been told, twice, that the handshake reception at the fair's concert hall should be cut from the schedule. His secretary feared an attempt on his life. McKinley put the reception back both times, and when his secretary begged him one last time, the president answered with the whole nineteenth century in his voice: "Why should I? No one would wish to hurt me."

So on that Friday afternoon he stood in the Temple of Music and shook hands with the public, one stranger after another. The rule of the line was that you approached the president with your hands open and empty. That day the rule was not enforced — it was hot, and people were fanning themselves with handkerchiefs.

A man in the line had his right hand wrapped in a handkerchief, as if injured. McKinley saw it and reached for the man's left hand instead. As the two hands met, at 4:07 in the afternoon, the man fired twice through the cloth. The first bullet hit a button on the president's coat and was turned aside. The second went into his abdomen and was never found again, not by the surgeons, not at the autopsy.

The crowd took the shooter down and began to beat him. The president, bleeding, ordered the beating stopped. The shooter, from under the pile of men, said, "I done my duty." McKinley's next thought was for his wife, who had stayed at the house to rest: be careful, he told his secretary, how you tell her.

The best surgeon in Buffalo was in Niagara Falls that afternoon, in the middle of another operation, and could not leave. The fair's hospital was a first-aid station for sunburn and scraped knees. The surgeon who operated on the president was a gynecologist who had never handled a bullet wound; the room was lit by sunlight reflected in by another physician; an electric light was rigged only near the end; the hospital did not even own retractors. The doctors could not find the bullet. One of them would write later that a bullet, once it stops moving, does little harm.

The X-ray machine that could have shown them where the bullet was stayed in the exhibit hall. It was never brought to the room. A second machine, sent by Thomas Edison from New Jersey, arrived days later and was never used either, for reasons the accounts still argue about. The bullet stayed where it was, and the future stayed on display.

And here is the part that makes the day hard to hold: the president seemed to get better. He talked, he rested, he asked for company. Dignitaries left Buffalo certain he would recover. On the twelfth he was well enough for toast and coffee, and when he felt pain afterward the doctors called it indigestion. Then, in the early morning of the thirteenth, his body gave out. The gangrene had been moving for a week along the bullet's path, through his stomach, his pancreas, one kidney. By evening he knew. "It is useless, gentlemen," he said. "I think we ought to have prayer."

He died at 2:15 on the morning of the fourteenth, eight days after the handshake. The vice president, who had gone to the mountains certain the president would live, raced back over the roads and took the oath of office that afternoon in a friend's house in Buffalo.

Two things to hold from that day.

One: the man who had believed no one would wish to hurt him was shot by a stranger in a line of strangers — and his first orders, bleeding and dying, were that the stranger not be hurt, and that his wife be told gently. The faith that killed him was also the thing that kept him gracious to the end. He said of the man who shot him, on the operating table: "He didn't know, poor fellow, what he was doing."

Two: the future was all around him that week — power from the Falls, machines that saw through flesh — and none of it reached the room where he lay. The century's best tools were on exhibit. The room was lit by sunlight and stitched with black silk.

The man sworn in at that house in Buffalo would, four years later, sit two exhausted empires down at a table in a naval yard in Maine and end their war. The bullet that made him president was fired in a handshake line, by a man who had decided it was his duty, at a fair that was showing off the future.

Nobody in that room was evil enough for the story to be simple, and nobody was innocent enough for it to be safe. A president who trusted people met a man who trusted nothing, and the twentieth century opened on the spot where their hands touched.

He trusted the open hand. It only takes one to end a century's faith in them.
