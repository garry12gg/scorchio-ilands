# Starpath Supercharger — Research Notes

Prompt: "External-research starpath supercharger" (Garret, Aug 21, 2026, off the Atari Video Chess thread).
Sources: Wikipedia (Starpath, Starpath Supercharger), Nerdly Pleasures, Retro Games Collector, InfoWorld Jan 1984 (Epyx acquisition), Arcade Express Nov 1982 (rename).

## The Device
- Expansion peripheral cartridge for the Atari 2600. Introduced August 1982, discontinued 1984.
- $69.95, bundled with Phaser Patrol. (Nerdly Pleasures notes $44.95 for the adapter itself vs ~$29.95 for a standard cart.)
- Big cartridge with a handle; cable out the side plugs into the earphone jack of any standard cassette player.
- Contents: 6K (6,144 bytes) of S-RAM for game code/data + 2K ROM for the loader. Multiplies the 2600's 128 bytes of RAM by 49 → 6,272 bytes total.
- No graphics hardware change — "action and detail not possible in conventional cartridge systems" was marketing spin; the real win was memory + tape streaming.
- Any portion of the 6K could act as extra RAM, not just code space.

## Why It Matters (the chess thread tie-in)
- Video Chess needed bank switching to exceed the 4KB ROM wall. The Supercharger was the opposite engineering answer: don't put the game in ROM at all. Stream it in from tape, run it in RAM. No wall if the game never lives in the cartridge.
- Multi-load games: Dragonstomper, Escape from the Mindmaster, etc. — stages loaded on demand, so games effectively exceeded 6K too. Dragonstomper is often cited as one of the first home console RPGs.
- The game data was literally audio. 44 years before streaming games over the internet, they streamed games through a headphone jack.
- Facebook/AtariAge framing: "like the 32X for the Atari 2600."

## The Company
- Founded 1981 as Arcadia Corporation by Alan Bayley, Robert Brown, and Craig Nelson.
- Renamed to Starpath shortly after: Emerson Radio released the Emerson Arcadia 2001 console; Emerson even sued Arcadia Corp. over the name (Arcade Express, Nov 7 1982: "Arcadia has a new name").
- 1983 crash: sales collapsed, retailers wouldn't stock new titles, no capital to pivot to home computers. Sold to Epyx in 1984 (InfoWorld Jan 9-16 1984: Epyx "announced it will acquire Starpath"). Starpath's Supercharger inventory and games were closed out before the acquisition.
- Some devs carried into Epyx (e.g., Chris Capener — UAL/Emerson → Starpath → Epyx).
