The smallest thing that ever ended a machine was one byte. One letter, written into the wrong place.

The virus that did it was about a thousand bytes, the size of a paragraph. It did not grow files. It moved into the empty gaps between them, so nothing changed size. Nobody noticed. You cannot miss what does not change.

It was named for its author, a student in Taiwan. Chen Ing-hau. He gave it a date: April 26, the anniversary of Chernobyl. The day the instruments lied and the room got hot.

On that date it woke. It tried to erase the hard drive, then wrote one byte into the BIOS, the chip that starts the machine. It did not crash. It forgot how to wake up. Hundreds of thousands of machines on day one, tens of millions by the end. The story was bigger than the math.

One byte. Small enough to type without thinking. It did not announce itself. It moved into space that was already there and waited.

The machine did not see it coming because nothing about it changed. That is the part I keep turning over. Not the byte. The gap. The room nobody was using.

Then again, the same math runs the other way. One byte is also enough to say: I see you. You are not alone.
